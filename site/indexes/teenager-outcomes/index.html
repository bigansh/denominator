01 / Construction

Seven dimensions, split evenly between now and next

Four dimensions measure the conditions of adolescence itself and total 50 points. Three measure the launchpad into adulthood and total the other 50. Indicators are normalised against the 5th and 95th percentile of the sample, so a single outlier cannot flatten the distribution.

Index = 0.5 × Life Score + 0.5 × Future Score. Percentile = share of the sample at or below.

02 / Method

How a score gets computed

Each indicator is clipped at its sample’s 5th and 95th percentile, rescaled 0–100, and inverted where lower is better. The bounds are frozen on the central values, so a low/high run moves a country against a fixed scale rather than reshaping the scale underneath it:

positive indicator100 × (clip(x) − p05) / (p95 − p05)
negative indicator100 × (p95 − clip(x)) / (p95 − p05)

Indicators roll into seven dimensions by fixed sub-weights (§01). Dimensions roll into Life Score (health, mental, safety, living — 50 pts) and Future Score (education, economic, digital — 50 pts), kept apart and averaged evenly so a comfortable present can’t hide a poor launchpad.

Every observation carries a value_low/value_high. The build runs the whole index three times — central, every country at its most favourable defensible value, every country at its least — and publishes the spread as the country’s band. That band is how a contested indicator (§04) gets handled: dropping it smuggles in a judgement, keeping it silent pretends to a precision the data doesn’t have.

Two percentiles are published for every country — one country, one unit; one teenager, one unit (worked through with India as the example in §06). For a demographically enormous country the population percentile uses the midpoint convention: the share of adolescents scoring strictly below, plus half the share tied in the country’s own block. Full derivation, including why the midpoint and not the endpoints, in the full derivation ↗.

03 / Sources & limits

What’s measured, what’s modelled, what’s missing

Every one of the 2,250 observations behind this index carries a source and a confidence tier. Open any row in the table (§05) to see an individual country’s sources; the tier legend and the caveats that apply across the board are below.

A
Physically counted or measured, nationally sourced, not seriously disputed — under-5 mortality, stunting, electricity access, homicide.
B
Measured, but with real comparability problems across countries — youth NEET, child labour, learning outcomes, mobile broadband.
C
Perceptual or modelled — the Cantril life evaluation, and any indicator imputed rather than observed.

Sources are organisation-level today — UN IGME, UNICEF/WHO/World Bank JME, WHO Global Health Estimates and NCRB for India, UNODC, ILO/UNICEF, WHO/UNICEF JMP, World Bank/IEA, UNESCO UIS, Altinok, Angrist & Patrinos via World Bank HLO, ILOSTAT, ITU, and the World Happiness Report — correctly attributed but not yet a specific series ID and access date on every row. Tightening those 2,250 citations is open work; see §10.

Mental & social wellbeing is the weakest dimension: suicide mortality and adult life evaluation are both proxies, and no adolescent-specific mental-health series has global country coverage. Bullying, school violence and digital-skills indicators were dropped for lack of coverage. Internet and electricity access are population-level proxies, not adolescent-specific. Within-India gradients (§09) are encoded from published NFHS-5 and PLFS patterns, not a microdata reanalysis, and bands above the 90th wealth percentile are extrapolated.

Sample covers 1.239 billion adolescents, about 95% of the world’s 10–19 population, in 125 of 194 countries — inclusion requires a complete row, so partial coverage means exclusion rather than imputation. Percentiles are within-sample. Full column-by-column schema in the data dictionary ↗.

04 / Contested

Four indicators, argued in the open

A contested indicator is a reason to publish a range, not a reason to drop the indicator or the conclusion it points to. Dropping it silently smuggles in a judgement — usually the loudest one. Full policy and worked cases in the contested-indicators policy ↗.

Life evaluation (Cantril ladder)Tier C

India scores far below its income level on this single Gallup World Poll question — an adult measure, not an adolescent one, and one where scale use and rising aspirations both plausibly depress the number as conditions actually improve. Handled with a ±20% band spanning WHR 2024–2026 (4.054–4.536).

Central, as published55.6rank 81
Ladder forced to the sample median57.8rank 80
Ladder deleted from the index entirely56.0rank 85

Deleting the indicator makes India’s rank worse, not better — the mental dimension then rests solely on suicide mortality, where India is also below median. The conclusion survives the dispute.

Stunting prevalenceTier A

The Government of India has rejected the Global Hunger Index in successive editions and cites Poshan Tracker administrative data showing lower wasting. This index doesn’t use the GHI — it uses NFHS-5 directly, India’s own probability-sample survey. The government’s objection is to the GHI’s construction, not to NFHS anthropometry; Poshan Abhiyaan is itself built around reducing the same measure, and the Tracker is a self-selected administrative census, not a substitute for a probability sample. A ±6% band spans the residual disagreement.

Youth NEET & the NEET gender gapTier B

Comparability, not accuracy: in low-income settings, subsistence farming and unpaid family work count as employment, which mechanically depresses measured NEET elsewhere. India’s value sits at the winsorisation ceiling and the gender gap exceeds the clipping bound outright, so India scores a literal zero on it — the floor is holding the index up, not the dispute pulling it down. ±5% band; the caveat is printed alongside the value rather than buried.

Harmonised learning outcomeTier B, C where imputed

Vintage 2005–2015, imputed for 29 of 125 countries by regression on under-5 mortality, youth literacy, completion and internet use (R² 0.87). China’s value derives from PISA-participating provinces, not the national population, and mainland China produced no PISA 2022 result at all. Imputed cells drop to tier C with a ±6% band. Replacing this series with something current is the single highest-value upgrade available to this repository.

06 / The disagreement

India, counted two ways

The index and the method are the same for every country in the table above. What follows takes India specifically as the worked example of what that method actually surfaces.

One country, one vote
35.5
India ranks 81st of 125 countries. Denmark, with 600,000 teenagers, counts the same as India with 253 million.
One teenager, one vote
42.9
Weight every country by its 10–19 population and India rises seven points, because the countries it outranks are demographically enormous.

Both numbers are correct. The first answers “how does India compare to other states?” The second answers “how does an Indian teenager compare to other teenagers?” Most published country rankings quietly answer the first while being read as the second.

07 / The case

Five dimensions at the median. Two in freefall.

India by dimension — bar is India, hairline is the sample median

India’s Living, Safety, Health, Education and Digital scores sit between the 42nd and 50th percentile — unremarkable for its income level, and improving. Mental & social wellbeing ranks 113th of 125. Economic future ranks 122nd.

Remove those two dimensions and India is a median country. They are the entire deficit.

Youth NEET of 39% sits at the winsorisation ceiling. The 32-point gap between female and male NEET exceeds the clipping bound, so India scores a literal zero on it. Together those two indicators forfeit 22 of 22.5 available points. The index cannot represent India as worse than this — the floor is holding the score up.

Where the Future Score goes — points earned of points available

Completion earns 85% of its available credit; learning outcome earns 33%. India moves adolescents through lower secondary at above-median rates and gets below-median cognitive output. Separating the two is what the framework exists to do.

09 / Disaggregation

Where the top of India actually lands

Split India by household wealth using domestic survey gradients, score each stratum on the same global scale, and drop the strata back into the world distribution. A claim like “Indian teenagers do better than 70% of the world” can then be tested rather than asserted.

India by wealth decile — world percentile reached
What is true, and for how many

No Indian wealth stratum clears the 75th world percentile. Wealth buys health, connectivity, schooling and safety; it does not buy out the Economic Future constraint, and youth unemployment actually rises with wealth and education in India. The richest decile still scores 79.1 on Life against 67.6 on Future.

India by state — world percentile reached

State spread runs 48 to 68; wealth spread runs 35 to 73. A state average pools all classes, so geography understates the real dispersion. No Indian state reaches the world’s 62nd percentile.

10 / Contribute

Fix a number, argue a weight, add a country

Corrections are the point of this repository, and you don’t need to touch any code to make one. Find a row in data/observations.csv, replace the value with a cited figure from the actual source, open a pull request. CI checks the schema and rebuilds this page from it. A weight or framework objection goes through an issue instead, with the before/after table for whichever countries move.
